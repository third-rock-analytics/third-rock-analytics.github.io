First clients

Every new practice faces the same bind: potential clients want to see your work, but you need clients to do work. This is how I started breaking that loop.

Jeff Zivkovic · 4 min read

Starting a solo practice, the first problem isn't technical. It's the portfolio loop: no client work to show, so no clients, so no client work. I'd seen it described before but assumed it was something you just pushed through. It turns out the loop is real and it's structural, not a confidence problem you can talk yourself out of.

The work I could show was academic, internal to past employers, or practice projects I'd built to learn something. All of it demonstrated that I knew the tools. None of it looked like someone had paid me to solve their problem. Those are different things, and clients can tell.

The cold start

The honest version of a new practice's portfolio: here are some maps I made, here is some analysis I ran, here is a web tool I built. All of it is real and representative of the work I'd do for you. But the gap between "representative" and "I hired this person and they delivered" is significant when someone is deciding whether to write a check.

Outreach to potential clients without a track record mostly produces polite silence. Not hostility — people aren't mean about it — but the response rate is low and the conversations that do happen tend to stall when it comes time to talk about past projects. You can compensate for a while with a clear website, a specific services list, and good answers to questions. But you can't fake a client reference, and you can't manufacture the kind of trust that comes from a third party saying "they delivered."

Non-profits as a starting point

The approach that worked was to stop treating the first project as a revenue opportunity and start treating it as a portfolio investment. I looked for non-profits with a clear spatial problem they couldn't afford to solve with a contractor. The trade was explicit: a real deliverable on a real problem, at no charge, in exchange for a project I could put in a portfolio and a reference I could point to.

Non-profits are a reasonable fit for this because the need is genuine. Environmental groups, land trusts, and community organizations often have GIS questions — where are our service gaps, what does our coverage look like on a map, can we visualize this field data — that go unanswered because they don't have the budget for a consultant and don't have the in-house capacity to do the work. The spatial problem is real. The constraint is money, not motivation.

There's also a scope discipline that comes with free work. A small non-profit with no budget has no incentive to let scope creep run wild. You agree on a deliverable, you build it, you hand it over. The project has edges, which is exactly what you want for a first engagement.

Finding the right fit

Not every non-profit is a useful early client. The ones worth approaching have a few things in common:

  • A specific spatial question, not a vague interest in "using GIS more." Service area analysis, site selection, field data collection and visualization — something with a clear output.
  • A point of contact who can give real feedback and sign off on the work. Committees and boards slow everything down and make it hard to know when you're done.
  • A deliverable that can go in a portfolio. If the data is sensitive or the output can't be shared, you've done the work but not solved the portfolio problem.

Finding these organizations takes more searching than you'd expect. Local land trusts, watershed councils, community development non-profits, and municipal-adjacent groups are good places to start. The pitch is short: here's what I do, here's what I'd build for you, here's what I'm asking for in return. Most don't respond. The ones that do tend to be eager, because the need is real and the price is right.

What free work is and isn't

Pro bono work is a tool, not a business model. The limit I set was one project: defined scope, defined deliverable, clear end date. The goal was to produce one portfolio item and one reference, then stop. Doing multiple free projects in a row just delays the transition to paid work without adding proportionally more evidence.

What you're actually buying with free work isn't the client reference — though that matters. It's the reps. Running a real project with a real stakeholder who has real opinions about the output is different from building something on your own. The feedback loop is tighter, the constraints are less forgiving, and the things that go wrong are the things that were actually going to go wrong. You find out whether the tools hold up, whether your process makes sense, and whether you can communicate clearly with someone who doesn't know what a shapefile is.

The non-profit work didn't directly produce paying clients. What it produced was a portfolio item I could point to and, more usefully, the confidence that I could deliver on a real project with real constraints. That changes how you write proposals and how you talk about the work. The portfolio loop broke not because a client fell out of the sky, but because I stopped waiting for one.
